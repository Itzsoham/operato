Index · neutral shell

Operato · design directions · 25 Aug 2026

Three redesigns of the same product.

These are not three colour schemes. Each one is a complete rebuild of the shell, the layout, the component forms and the visual language — a different sidebar, a different header, a different card, a different type system, a different idea of how much should fit on a screen. Nothing here is the stock shadcn dashboard with new hex values.

So the choice in front of you is not "which palette do we like". It is which personality does this product have — because whichever one you pick, every screen Operato ever ships gets built from its contract. Pick the room it lives in, the person reading it, and how loud it is allowed to be.

01

What you are actually choosing

Each direction ships with a written contract — a spec comment at the head of its dashboard file that every other screen is built from. Choosing a direction means adopting that contract.

The shell changes

The sidebar is an opaque charred slab in one, a flat panel of pale wood in the next and blurred glass over aubergine in the third. The header is a hero band with an ember glow, a full-bleed paper wash, or a brass-bracketed frame. There is no shared chrome underneath — swapping directions later is a rewrite, not a re-theme.

The component forms change

A KPI tile is a perforated paper docket, a ceramic card on a hairline, or a brass-ruled panel with an inner sheen. A ranked list is a hard-outlined row, a dotted-leader menu line, or a brass rail with lozenge nodes. Buttons are 10px chunky rectangles, 10px soft rectangles, or 2px near-square blocks.

The density changes

The same eight screens hold roughly a third more rows in one direction than in another, because whitespace budgets differ by design. That is a product decision about how much a manager should see at once — it is not a styling detail, and it does not survive a later "let's just tighten it up".

02

What is identical in all three

The trade-offs below are about personality only. None of the three trades away rigour to get its look — these constants are held in every file.

  • The same eight screensOverview, Menu, Orders + floor grid, Inventory, Customers, Staff, Ask AI, Billing — over the same tenant-scoped data model.
  • The same responsive ladderFull sidebar ≥ 1280, icon rail 1024–1279, off-canvas drawer plus a five-slot bottom tab bar ≤ 1023. Rail labels are clipped, never display:none.
  • The same contrast floorBody text clears 4.5:1 against the surface it actually sits on, textures composited in. Every ramp splits a fill step from a text step, and the faintest step is documented as decorative strokes only.
  • Status is never colour aloneEvery state prints a word, and sequence states also print a five-step meter. Every delta prints an arrow glyph and a signed number.
  • One solid chip, spent onceEach direction allows exactly one solid-filled chip in the whole system and spends it on READY — the thing someone must find at arm's length across a room.
  • Tablet-first touch targets44 px minimum on every interactive element; Forno raises its own floor to 46 px. These run on a pass, on a counter, with wet hands.
  • Indian money formattingLakh grouping, no paise, tabular figures, the ₹ glyph set smaller and lighter than its digits so money columns still align: ₹1,47,850, ₹20,35,350.
  • Hand-authored inline SVG chartsNo chart library in any direction, and an incomplete period is always drawn dashed and labelled — no direction lets a running day look finished.
  • The FSSAI marks A filled dot inside a square outline, green for veg and maroon for non-veg. A legal convention, not a design token — identical in every direction and never tinted to match a palette.
  • One honest discrepancy to settleThe three files drifted on the exact legal hexes — veg is #0B7A34 / #1A7F3C / #1B7F3B and non-veg is #94231A / #8B1E1E / #7A1E1E. Whichever direction wins, freeze one pair in globals.css and stop theming it.

Direction A

Forno

A wood-fired counter at 8:40 on a Saturday night. Loud, warm, fast, a little hand-made — a charred oven mouth over a bench dusted with semolina.

Signature moves

  • The charred slab and the crust edge. Sidebar and hero share one recipe — brick mortar grid, leopard blisters, a flickering ember glow — and every dark band is cut off in a row of bubbled crust circles instead of a border.
  • The docket. KPI tiles are paper receipts: a 7px colour bar, a 2px drawn outline, a scorched corner and a bitten-out perforated bottom edge.
  • Hard shadows that press. 5px 5px 0 black on every card and button, and :active translates the object into its own shadow. Nothing floats; everything is stuck to a wall.

Best at

  • Being read fast, from a distance, under pressure. Hierarchy is carried by weight and hard black outline rather than by subtle tint, so it survives glare, grease and a glance.
  • Density. It is the only one of the three that wants a busy screen. Thirty live orders on a floor grid look intentional here and cramped elsewhere.
  • Touch. 46px targets and the press-translate give physical feedback on a tablet with no haptics — the strongest tactile feel of the three.
  • Being unmistakable. Nobody will ever say this looks like a template.

What it costs

  • Calm. 900-weight uppercase everywhere is tiring across an eight-hour shift, and there is nowhere quiet to put a low-stakes screen — Billing shouts as loudly as the pass.
  • Typographic predictability. The display face is Arial Black with an Impact fallback; it renders noticeably differently across Windows, macOS and Android.
  • Build and maintenance. Speckle, brick, leopard, scorch, the crust mask and the perforated docket (which needs filter: drop-shadow, not box-shadow) make it roughly two to three times the component work of Crema.
  • Reuse. It is emphatically a restaurant. When Operato takes its platform seam to vertical #2, this direction does not travel.

Direction B

Crema

A well-run cafe at 7am. Calm, airy, precise — a wiped counter, warm morning light, pale wood and ceramic. Whitespace is the primary material.

Signature moves

  • The counter rule. Every masthead closes on three stacked hairlines with 3px gaps instead of a shadow, and every section head carries a double-hairline rule that runs to the far edge. It is why a screen reads like a printed price list.
  • The dot-leader board. Any name paired with a number becomes a chalkboard row: rank square, FSSAI mark, name, a dotted leader, count, price. The single most reusable component of the three.
  • Light, large figures. Every headline number is weight 300 at up to 44px, tabular; labels track at .18em. Numbers are never bold in this theme, and there is exactly one pill on any screen.

Best at

  • Long sessions. The least fatiguing of the three for someone actually reading — reconciling stock, reviewing a week, working through the AI answers.
  • Cost and durability. Flat paper, hairlines, no backdrop-filter, no masks, no textures. Roughly a week of component work, it degrades gracefully, and it will not look dated in 2030.
  • Travelling to vertical #2. Strip the coffee words and it is a competent operating screen for a salon or a pharmacy. Given Operato's platform ambition, this matters more than it looks like it does.
  • Print and export. A light paper ground is the only one that survives a PDF or a printed shift sheet without rework.

What it costs

  • Distinctiveness. Restraint sits one bad sprint away from generic. If a developer skips the .18em labels, the dot leaders and the triple rule, it collapses straight back into the stock shadcn dashboard we are trying to leave.
  • Distance legibility. Weight-300 numerals are elegant up close and the weakest of the three across a counter in glare — the exact opposite of Forno's trade.
  • Density. The whitespace budget is explicitly non-negotiable downwards and costs you roughly a third of the rows per screen.
  • Emphasis headroom. One espresso card per screen means a screen with two genuinely urgent things has nowhere to put the second.

Signature moves

  • Brass brackets and the double rule. Four L-shaped 1px hairlines frame whatever is being presented, and a brass double rule closes the hero and separates section heads. Both are rationed to a hard per-screen budget.
  • The bone card. Exactly one inverted linen-white island per screen, carrying that screen's most human content. It is the tablecloth, and there is never a second one.
  • Small-caps brass labels on near-black. Every label is 10.5px, .20em, brass — that single decision does more theming work than the rest of the palette combined. Radii top out at 10px; there are no pills.

Best at

  • Low-light rooms. The only one of the three that does not blast a host stand or a pass at 9pm. In a dim dining room this is not a preference, it is the correct answer.
  • Perceived value. Serif display, brass hairlines and squared geometry read as expensive. It is the easiest of the three to put a higher price on.
  • A few large numbers. Built for a 57-cover house: figures are few, large and given room. An owner reviewing the night's takings is its ideal reader.
  • Discipline. Its ornament budget is the tightest, so it degrades the most gracefully when a hurried developer adds a screen.

What it costs

  • A dark ground is a permanent constraint. Uploaded menu photos, the Razorpay checkout iframe, exported PDFs and any white third-party asset all fight it, and every one of them needs handling — forever, on every screen.
  • Daylight. A tablet on a counter beside a window is its worst case, and that is a real Operato tenant.
  • A legal workaround you carry forever. The FSSAI maroon measures 1.66:1 on this ground, so the mark has to be drawn on its own white tile on every screen. Correct, documented, and still a permanent asterisk.
  • Volume. Low-count numeracy is a design premise, not a setting. A 900-order-a-day canteen tenant will strain this direction hard.

05

How to choose

Answer these about the real tenant, not the pitch deck. Four of the five usually point the same way; when they do, that is the answer.

Where is the tablet, and what is the light like?

This is the question that decides the ground, and the ground is the one thing you cannot blend later. A bright counter next to a window needs a light ground. A dim dining room at 9pm needs a dark one, and a light screen there is genuinely unpleasant.

Bright · Forno Bright · Crema Dim · Saffron

Who reads it, and for how long?

A line cook glancing for three seconds is served by weight and hard outline. A manager reading for forty minutes is served by whitespace. An owner reviewing at close is served by a few large figures with room around them.

3 seconds · Forno 40 minutes · Crema At close · Saffron

How many rows must fit on the busiest screen?

Thirty live orders on a floor grid look intentional in Forno and cramped in Crema. Eight checks look composed in Saffron and sparse in Forno. Count the real rows on the real busiest night before answering.

30+ rows · Forno 15 rows · Crema 8 rows · Saffron

What is Operato selling in eighteen months?

The platform seam exists so vertical #2 can be something other than food. If that is a salon or a pharmacy, Crema travels and Forno does not. If the plan is to stay in restaurants and go upmarket, that constraint costs nothing and Saffron is the stronger asset.

Vertical #2 · Crema Upmarket · Saffron

What can the team build, and keep building?

Crema is roughly a week of component work and stays correct when someone rushes. Saffron adds glass, glows and one permanent legal workaround. Forno's masks, textures and drop-shadow filters are two to three times the work and the least forgiving to extend.

Cheapest · Crema Middle · Saffron Dearest · Forno

Who has to be impressed in the first ten seconds?

If it is an investor or an upmarket owner, Saffron reads as the most expensive product in the room. If it is the person who will actually use it during service, Forno wins the demo and Crema wins the second week.

Investor · Saffron Demo · Forno Week two · Crema

One thing you cannot do: split the difference

You can lift a component across directions — Crema's dot-leader board would work in any of the three, and the five-step status meter already appears in all of them. What you cannot do is run two shells. The dark-ground decision propagates into charts, uploaded images, the Razorpay checkout iframe, PDF exports, the FSSAI mark and every empty state. Pick the ground first; everything else is downstream of it.

Whichever direction wins, the next step is the same: take its spec comment as the contract, build the remaining five screens — Inventory, Customers, Staff, Ask AI, Billing — from it without re-deriving a single value, then freeze the tokens into src/app/globals.css.

Nine files, three directions. Every screen is one self-contained HTML file with its tokens declared on :root, inline SVG icons, no CDN, no external font and no remote asset — open any of them straight off disk.
